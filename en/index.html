Why it slows down

The fan spins, but the culprit stays hidden

When a Mac slows down we go looking for the heaviest app. On a Mac running several AI tools, the culprit isn’t at the top of the list — it isn’t in the list at all.

The heavy work finished long ago, and the fan is still spinning.

Only a few windows are open, yet scrolling feels sticky and switching apps lags.

“Your system has run out of application memory.” You quit the app it named, and minutes later it’s back.

You open Activity Monitor, see hundreds of rows, and have no idea where to start.

When these four arrive together, it’s usually not one big app but hundreds of small processes piling up. Each one is harmless on its own — which is exactly why neither the warning nor Activity Monitor can point at them.

“Can’t I just count them on my own Mac?”

Not in one line. Counting parentless processes on the Mac this was written on gives you this:

$ ps -eo ppid= | awk '$1==1' | wc -l 798

All ~800 of them have “no parent”, and the number worth cleaning up is zero. Spotify, Notion, Safari’s tabs and dozens of Apple’s audio helpers all have launchd as their parent too. Having no parent isn’t evidence of being orphaned — it’s just an absence of information. Telling real orphans apart from processes that are doing their job — that is what Paws does.

What it finds

Tells you why it's slow, by name

Plenty of tools show you how much memory something uses. Paws goes further and decides which of them lost its owner.

01

Orphan processes

A process left behind when its parent died. It becomes a candidate only once three or more of the same command have piled up. A lone one is left alone.

02

Large processes with no session

Something no terminal is holding that still eats a lot of memory. Paws never ends it on its own; it only lists it.

03

Memory pressure

The ring in the menu bar says the state in color. Grey when calm, orange on warning, red on danger. A graph shows how it got there.

How it works

It doesn’t guess.
It asks the kernel

What to keep is the harder question, not what to remove. Paws narrows that judgment in four steps, and stops at any step where it isn’t sure.

01

It watches

Memory pressure and swap every 5 seconds, the process list every 15. All you see is one ring in the menu bar.

NO EFFECT ON YOUR WORKPaws stays under 100 MB. It remembers the last 15 minutes and drops the rest.

02

It judges

It asks “is a session holding this?”, not “is this busy?”. It reads the group the kernel put in front of the terminal (e_tpgid), and protection spreads upward to the parent.

NO EFFECT ON YOUR WORKWhatever the terminal holds, and everything beneath it, is protected — including an AI session sitting at 0% CPU waiting for input.

03

It narrows

Only commands stacked three deep or more become candidates. Apple’s XPC services and app extensions are never candidates at all.

NO EFFECT ON YOUR WORKA lone process may simply be working. When it isn’t sure, it doesn’t list it.

04

You choose

It opens the record window, shows you what is about to end and asks again. For processes no session holds, the list opens with nothing ticked at all. What was ended, when and why is kept for a year.

NO EFFECT ON YOUR WORKAutomatic cleanup is off by default. Even switched on, it only touches orphans that passed step 03.

We got this judgment wrong once

The first version treated “not in launchctl’s list” as proof of being orphaned. On that rule it ended 108 healthy Apple XPC services — Safari’s web content processes among them. It shipped with all 100 synthetic tests passing. Absence from the list was an absence of information, not evidence, and testing against fake processes could never have revealed that.

Now a test on a real machine — “on a healthy Mac, is the automatic cleanup set empty?” — runs on every build. The rules have grown to six since that day, and every one of them defaults to doing nothing when unsure.

What it never does

A tool that loses your work
isn't worth using

What matters most in a cleanup app is not what it removes but what it leaves.

  • HOLD

    Never touches what your terminal is holding

    Everything hanging below it is protected too. An AI session waiting for input counts.

  • HOLD

    Nothing is pre-checked

    The cleanup list opens empty. Every process that ends is one a person picked.

  • HOLD

    Data never leaves this machine

    No usage statistics, no crash reports. The license check is the single exception.

  • HOLD

    No termination without a record

    It asks again before ending anything, and writes down what ended, when, and why. When the call is uncertain, it does nothing.

Zero CPU doesn't mean idle. Claude Code waiting for input uses 0 CPU. Cleanup tools that judge by CPU kill it. Paws reads the group the kernel put in front of the terminal, e_tpgid. It asks whether a session owns it, not whether it's busy.
